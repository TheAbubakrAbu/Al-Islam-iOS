A: The Quran presents Ayat al-Kursi as a declaration of Allah’s sovereignty and perfect knowledge. Allah’s words in Surah Al-Baqarah, verse 2:255 [1], state that He is the Ever-Living, the Sustainer of all existence, and that nothing can stand between Him and creation except by His permission. The same verse also emphasizes that His knowledge covers everything that is present and everything that will come, and that nothing exists beyond His will.

The Prophet is said to have described this verse as the greatest of all. In a report by Imam Ahmad, Ubayy bin Kab mentioned that the Prophet asked about the greatest verse and answered with Ayat al-Kursi [3]. Another account from Jami` at-Tirmidhi, narrated by 'Abdullah bin Mas'ud, adds that no creation in heaven or earth is more magnificent than this verse [6].

The Tafsir of Ibn Kathir highlights the virtues linked to Ayat al-Kursi, noting that authentic traditions speak of its tremendous benefits [3]. The Tafsir of Maarif Ul Quran also calls it the greatest verse, connecting its merits to broader spiritual gains [1].

The Quran itself reinforces the idea that Allah’s knowledge and power extend beyond the heavens and the earth, as seen in the same verse [7]. This theme is further grounded in the declaration that He is the Most High and the Most Great [4].

A: The verse you asked about is 1:4 from Surah Al-Fatihah, a surah from the Makkan period [1]. In your words, Allah is described as the Sovereign of the Day of Recompense, a concept tied to His absolute authority and power over all outcomes on that day [1].

The tafsir of Ibn Kathir explains that this mention of sovereignty on the Day of Resurrection does not take away from Allah’s sovereignty over everything else, He is the Lord of existence, including this life and the Hereafter [2]. The commentator also clarifies that the word ‘Malik’ comes from the root ‘milk,’ meaning possessing something with the right and power to decide its fate, and ‘Din’ means ‘Requital,’ so the verse underscores that no one except Him can claim ownership of anything on that day [1].

How the readings (qiraat) differ in this verse:

1. Khalaf, al-Kisai, Yaqub, and Asim read the word māliki yawmi d-dīni, meaning ‘Owner of the Day of Judgement,’ which reflects the idea of ownership and God’s ability to act freely [1].
2. Abu Jafar, Ibn Amir, Nafi, Hamzah, Ibn Kathir, and Abu Amr read maliki yawmi d-dīni, meaning ‘King of the Day of Judgement,’ which describes God’s authority and sovereignty [1].

No other evidence from the sources clarifies the broader context beyond these readings.

A: The Quran speaks about patience in several ways, linking it to faith and reliance on God. In Surah Al-Baqarah, Allah's words in verse 2:153 [1] say, “O you who have believed, seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, Allah is with the patient.” The same surah adds in verse 2:45 [6] that patience and prayer help, especially for those who humbly submit.

Surah Al-Ma'arij reinforces this in verse 70:5 [3] by stating, “So be patient with gracious patience.” Together, these verses show that patience is a central virtue sustained through trust in God.

No single verse in the Quran defines patience as a fixed quality, but these texts present it as a response to life’s trials and a path to inner peace.

A: Tawakkul is trusting in Allah while still taking the lawful steps you can. It is more than mere hope; it is a firm belief that nothing happens without His guidance.

The Quran makes it clear that true reliance is central to faith. In Surah Al-A'raf [3], Allah says, “Upon Allah we have relied,” and in Surah Ali 'Imran [4], He says, “Our Lord, decide between us and our people in truth, and You are the best of those who give decision.” These verses show that surrendering to His will is the foundation of trust.

The Prophet also spoke about this in ways that strengthen its meaning. In a hadith recorded by Imam Muslim [6], he said, “When you set of your trained dogs having recited the name of Allah, then eat what these (hounds) have caught for you, even if it (the game) is killed, provided (the hunting dog) has not eaten (any part of the game).” This shows that relying on Allah does not mean giving up effort, but rather trusting that the effort is in His hands. Another hadith from Jabir bin ‘Abdullah [5] adds that eating with “trust in Allah and reliance upon Allah” is a good practice, linking this trust to daily actions.

The app's articles explain that tawakkul is not about ignoring reality but about holding fast to the truth. In its discussion of tawhid [1], it says that actions like vowing or asking for rescue are worship only when done with tawakkul, meaning that directing them to anything but Allah is shirk. This shows that true reliance is tied to submission and clarity in belief.
